# LLM Eval & Observability — Conducive Sentences (prose retelling)

> Plain-English narration of the whole topic, section bridging to section. Read this to *talk* fluently, not just recall facts. Pairs with [`answers.md`](answers.md).

---

**Start with what eval even is.** Evaluating an LLM feature means turning its behavior on inputs you choose into a number or verdict you can compare across changes. The catch is that the output is open-ended text, so there's usually no single correct string to diff against — which is why the accuracy and F1 you'd reach for in classic ML don't transfer cleanly. You end up measuring *proxies* like faithfulness and relevancy, and every proxy can be gamed. That reality is exactly why the whole field splits into two loops.

**Those two loops are the spine of everything.** Offline eval runs before you deploy, against a fixed golden dataset, and its job is to *gate*: should this change ship? Online eval runs after you deploy, against sampled live traffic, and its job is to *monitor*: is what shipped still good? Neither replaces the other — offline is blind to inputs it never contained, and online only tells you after a user was affected. The loop finally closes when the hard cases production surfaces get harvested back into the golden set. If you remember one sentence, it's that one.

**Because offline leans on the golden set, that dataset is the first thing to get right.** A golden case is an input plus an expectation plus the scorers to apply plus tags — not necessarily a gold answer string. You bootstrap the first set from real questions you already have (logs, support tickets, docs-search terms), a handful of SME-seeded questions, and synthetic questions generated from known doc chunks; then every production incident becomes a permanent case. You keep it honest by owning it like code — a review cadence, a "last verified" date, and context pinned by stable doc id so a docs edit doesn't silently rot a case. And you stay suspicious of a high, flat score: if it's 0.95 while users complain, the set is probably unrepresentative or contaminated, not the system perfect.

**With a dataset in hand, you choose how to score each criterion.** The instinct is rule-first: anything objective or structural — JSON validity, a required citation, a safety pattern — should be a deterministic check, because it's free, instant, and never flaky, and because you never want a stochastic judge deciding safety. Reserve the expensive model-graded scorer for genuinely semantic questions. For RAG you split the metrics by layer: retrieval metrics (is the answer-bearing chunk in top-K, and is it ranked well?) versus generation metrics (is every claim grounded in the retrieved context, and does the answer actually address the question?). Faithfulness and answer relevancy are different axes — a grounded answer to the wrong question and a fluent hallucination are both failures, in different ways.

**When you do need a model to judge, treat it as an instrument, not an oracle.** It can score one output on a rubric (pointwise) or pick the better of two (pairwise), and pairwise tends to be the more reliable way to answer the question you usually care about: did this change improve things? Techniques like G-Eval help by making the judge decompose the criterion and reason before scoring. But judges have systematic biases — they favor whichever answer came first, they reward verbosity, they prefer their own model family, and they drift toward leniency — so you swap orders, normalize length, use a *different* model than the one under test, and force explicit fail criteria. Crucially, before a judge is allowed to block a merge, you validate it against human labels and you pin its version, because an unpinned judge is a measuring tape that quietly changes length.

**All of this becomes useful only when it runs automatically as a gate.** A harness like promptfoo gives you a declarative matrix of providers times prompts times tests, a library of assertion types, caching, and a CI reporter — everything you'd otherwise rebuild by hand. You keep the gate credible by threshold-ing on *regression versus a baseline* rather than a brittle absolute score, by removing avoidable randomness (temperature zero, pinned judge) and reporting pass rates over enough cases, and by tiering the work: fast rule checks on every PR against a cached subset, and the full golden set with the full judge matrix nightly. If the suite ever gets so slow and expensive that people skip it, it's protecting nothing — so you fix the cost, not the coverage.

**Offline can only ever see what you imagined, which is why online eval exists.** On live traffic you instrument each request as a trace of spans — rephrase, embed, retrieve, re-rank, answer — with tokens, latency, and ids per span, and with prompt and model *versions* as attributes, because otherwise you can't tell whether quality dropped from your change or the provider's. You can't afford to judge every request, so you sample a small percentage for async model-grading while mining the free implicit signals — thumbs, copies, immediate retries, abandons — on all of it; a spike in five-second retries is often a faster regression alarm than any judge. You watch three drifts separately, because they have different causes and different runbooks: input drift (users asking new things), quality drift (judge sample and thumbs-down worsening), and cost drift (tokens and dollars climbing). And you keep only the cheap, deterministic guardrails on the request path — PII patterns, schema validity, cost caps — pushing everything model-graded to the async side so you don't tax every call.

**The 2 AM incident is the scenario that ties observability together.** Answer quality degrades with no exception thrown — maybe the provider shipped a new model minor version, maybe the weekly re-embedding job half-failed and retrieval now returns stale or empty context. Users just see confidently-wrong or thin answers. Nothing pages you unless you've alerted on quality proxies and on job success, and you can only recover quickly if versions are pinned so you have something to roll back to. That's the whole argument for version discipline and for the design doc's insistence on backing up and validating new embeddings.

**Applied to RAG end-to-end, the master skill is attribution.** Given a wrong answer, you first ask whether the gold chunk was even in top-K; if not, it's a retrieval fault in chunking, embeddings, K, the re-ranker, or the query rephrase. If it *was* retrieved, you feed the known-good context straight to the model: if the answer becomes correct, retrieval ranking was to blame; if it's still wrong, it's a generation fault — the prompt, the model, faithfulness, or context lost in the middle of a long prompt. A custom re-ranker like Ask AI's (favoring medium chunks, bonusing code and headings) gets evaluated with rank-aware metrics and, honestly, with ablations — turn each heuristic off and see if quality actually drops, because a heuristic that moves no metric is just latency.

**That leads straight to a candid read of the shipped system.** Ask AI today evaluates with cosine similarity from an older embedding model plus a GPT-4 classifier into human-readable buckets, run from a Google Sheet — a perfectly reasonable *baseline*. Its three highest-value upgrades, in order, are to put it in CI as a regression gate, to split retrieval-versus-generation metrics so failures become attributable, and to validate the judge and control its biases. Those upgrades aren't hypothetical; they're precisely the first weeks of the hands-on build track.

**Finally, at staff level, eval stops being a script and becomes a governed system with a budget.** You centralize the harness, the judge service, and the standards, while letting feature teams own their golden sets and thresholds next to the prompts they gate. You budget eval like any recurring dependency because judge calls times matrix size times runs is real money, and you cache and sample to keep it bounded. For sensitive data you redact or synthesize cases and keep the judge inside your trust boundary — which is where deploying on Bedrock in a VPC earns its keep. Agents raise the bar again: you evaluate the whole trajectory and tool use, not just the final message. And you stay honest about the ways eval *lies* — Goodhart's law, contaminated datasets, a judge that's colluding because it's the same model as the subject — defending with rotating holdouts, a distinct validated judge, and constant correlation to real user signal. When the offline scores, the online proxies, and the cost curves disagree, you don't average them into one comfortable number; you report each with its meaning, lead with what real users are telling you, and name the disagreement out loud.
